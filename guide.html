02 · How to ask

The question does half the work. Ask well.

An oracle answers the question you actually put to it — not the one you meant. Four small habits, and you’ll never need them spelled out again.

1
Bring the one keeping you up

Not the safest question — the real one. The figure has the most to say about the thing you keep circling back to. Ask that.

2
Say it plainly, then give it a spine

One clear sentence, then a line or two of context: who’s involved, what’s at stake, the fork you’re at. Not a diary — just enough to bite on.

3
Ask about a movement, not a fact

“Should I take the offer?” reads far better than “will it rain Tuesday?” The instrument reasons about direction, timing and choice.

4
One honest cast, then sit with it

Re-casting the same question hoping for a kinder answer only buys you noise. Ask once, in good faith. If the answer stings, that’s often the part worth reading twice.

03 · What comes back

A figure is a map of forces — not a verdict, and rarely a single one.

What returns is not a yes-or-no, and not a fortune-cookie line. It is the shape of your situation: where the energy sits, which line is moving, which way the whole thing is tilting.

What it points to

The figure describes structure and motion — a configuration of forces and where they’re heading. Read it as terrain and weather, not a lottery number. It tells you the lay of the land and which way the wind blows; the walking is still yours.

Read it more than once

A good reading is layered. The same figure holds several true readings at once, depending on which thread of your life you lay it against — work, a person, your own nerve. Hold it up to each. The right one usually recognises itself.

Ambiguity is the honesty

When a reading holds more than one meaning, that isn’t hedging — it’s refusing to flatten a complicated life into a slogan. A single tidy answer to a genuinely open question would be the lie. Take the plural view as a gift: the door is still open.

04 · The one rule that outlives all the others

A good omen is not a finish line.

A favourable figure tells you the wind is with you — it does not row the boat. An unfavourable one names the cost early, while you can still afford to pay it. Either way the outcome isn’t delivered; it is earned. The oracle shows you the grain of the wood — you still do the carving.

The figure is cast by the old logic

The six lines and the hexagram they form come from a faithful casting engine — the same causal method, untouched, that dynasties reasoned by. No model chooses the figure. Chance and structure do, as they always have.

Claude finds the words — and only the words

The reading is written by Claude, held to the figure that was cast. The hexagram decides what may be said; the model finds a clear, human way to say it — and never overrules the casting to tell you what you’d rather hear. Ancient structure, modern voice.
